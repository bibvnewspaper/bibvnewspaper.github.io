## AAPI Month!
 
By Stephanie Song (8Pt)

The AAPI population is the fastest-growing ethnic group in the United States. Since the 2000s, the AAPI population has more than doubled, going from 12 million to about 26 million in just 26 years. All these AAPI households contribute billions into annual taxes and own countless businesses across the country that drive innovation in tech and services. Additionally, AAPI workers make up a big portion of many essential jobs and fill critical labor gaps. So, why is the AAPI population still heavily discriminated against and so underrepresented in the media?

Back in 2020, during COVID-19, attacks fueled by anti-Asian hate surged because China was linked to the virus. Verbal harassment, hate crimes, and vandalism were especially common and caused many Asian Americans to fear going out in public because of the violence. Furthermore, AAPI members often become seen as a monolith because of harmful stereotypes. They are often portrayed as quiet, intelligent, and successful in STEM, which heavily relates to the depiction that AAPI members are nerdy and socially awkward, which is not true at all. There is a common societal expectation that Asian Americans should become either a doctor, a lawyer, or an engineer, but that puts a lot of pressure on children and prevents them from doing something they actually want to do. All stereotypes, even positive ones, are harmful because they limit the dreams of many kids and erase the diversity of the AAPI community.

On social media like TikTok or Instagram, many trends blatantly disrespect Asian and Pacific Islander culture. For example, traditional Pacific Islander clothing, such as leis or hula, is worn for the aesthetics or cheapened by fast-fashion for social media posts. Western clothing brands have also sexualized traditional Asian clothing like qipaos, hanboks, and saris, and turned them into costumes for the internet. With the “You’ve met me at a very Chinese time of my life” trend, people are adopting Chinese culture, food, and life by making it a joke and not actually appreciating it. Some people even do this trend with other AAPI countries’ culture and label it as “Chinese”. In terms of food, some creators share AAPI dishes and don’t mention the culture behind them, and when their videos blow up and cause many people to try their recipe, they all claim the creator invented it, completely disregarding the history behind that recipe in the culture it was stolen from.

In other media, AAPI culture is often made fun of and exaggerated for entertainment. Yellowface casting for eastern Asians is extremely common, as well

 as overemphasizing accents (especially an Indian accent), which is essentially mockery. AAPI characters in movies often have side roles with nerdy, submissive, or villainous characteristics that reinforce stereotypes that fuel racism and the dehumanization of AAPI. Representation in film is especially important to inspire future AAPI individuals to become who they want to be.

Although AAPI individuals have been more represented recently, it is also important to talk about the representation within AAPI. When most people think of AAPI, they think of East Asia, India, and Hawaii. However, Asia and the Pacific Islands have much more diversity than that. The term AAPI is an umbrella term encompassing an extremely diverse demographic that spans over 50 ethnic groups with unique cultures, languages, traditions, and histories. There are 4 broad regions of the AAPI population: East Asia, Southeast Asia, South Asia, and the Pacific Islands & Native Hawaiians. It’s also interesting to me that when people say the term half-asians, they are almost always referring to wasians (white asians), but what about the other half-asians, like Hispanic Asians and black Asians? Where is the representation for them? In the AAPI community, shouldn’t we be fighting for equal representation for ALL Asians, just like we fought for representation in Western media? 

At the end of the day, as the AAPI community grows, we should focus on supporting each other in the community and uplifting each other. AAPI individuals shouldn’t be confined to a singular stereotype, but instead be seen as a diverse blend of centuries of history and culture. Speak up for what is right, even if it is hard. Happy AAPI Month!
