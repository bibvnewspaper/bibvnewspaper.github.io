## Hantavirus and Norovirus

By Megna Prakash(8Ti)

In Early May, on the cruise ship, MV Hondius, a rare virus rapidly spread and infected over a dozen individuals on board. The ship was later rerouted, and passengers were quarantined and closely monitored. The virus was identified shortly after as the Andes strand of the deadly Hantavirus. This wasn’t the first outbreak of Hantavirus, which was first recognized in Argentina in 1995, but spurred public health concern. The Andes Strand, caused by exposure or contact with infected rodents, is the only known strand of Hantavirus that spreads by person-to-person contact. 

Symptoms of Hantavirus are identified as “flu-like” in its early stages, and eventually transition to a more severe and life threatening respiratory and cardiovascular disease known as Hantavirus Pulmonary Syndrome, characterized by shortness of breath and fluid buildup in the lungs.

The Hantavirus was known to have spread when passengers from the MV Hondius travelled back to their home countries. In the United States, those who showed symptoms mimicking Hantavirus were closely monitored and quarantined. While public health officials have confirmed that there is minimal risk, some precautions you can take to avoid hantavirus is preventing rodent exposure, especially to wild deer mice. Remember to clean sheds or garages safely to avoid inhaling contaminated air.

Norovirus outbreaks have been widely reported, with origins similar to those of the Hantavirus, on a cruise ship in the Caribbean. Norovirus is not a zoonotic disease, meaning that it doesn't originate from animals. Norovirus is transmitted entirely through humans, and is characterized by symptoms that primarily affect the gastrointestinal tract.

So what is the deal with the recent outbreak of “cruise-ship viruses"? Cruise ships are known for having a large amount of people in close proximity, which multiplies the effect of viruses which spread by human-to-human contact.
